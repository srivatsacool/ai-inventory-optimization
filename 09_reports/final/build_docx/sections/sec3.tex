\section{Models Used}
\label{sec:models}
Each rung below states what the method does in one breath, why it earned a
place, how it was actually run here, and what job it does on the ladder.
Textbook derivations are omitted; implementation truth is in appendix C.

\subsection{Naive}
\label{sec:m-naive}
\emph{Does:} tomorrow equals today. \emph{Why:} the irreducible baseline ---
any method that cannot beat ``repeat yesterday'' adds no value.
\emph{Implementation:} last observed value carried forward 28 days; history
strictly before each origin. \emph{Role:} reference floor. It is a weak floor
(M5 MAE 1.18; Store 16.29) but, remarkably, wins 6 of 27 Store inventory
policies --- a first hint that accuracy and cost diverge.

\subsection{Seasonal Naive}
\label{sec:m-snaive}
\emph{Does:} tomorrow equals the same weekday last week. \emph{Why:} retail
demand has a weekly heartbeat (figure 7); this is the cheapest way to
use it. \emph{Implementation:} repeats only the last seven \emph{observed}
values (a lookahead bug reaching past the seasonal window was found and fixed
during hardening). \emph{Role:} seasonal reference line run in every phase. It
is Store's third-cheapest inventory driver (2,128.14) despite middling
accuracy --- weekly rhythm, it turns out, stocks shelves well.

\subsection{Moving Average}
\label{sec:m-ma}
\emph{Does:} forecasts the average of the last $w$ days --- a low-pass filter
on noisy demand. \emph{Why:} the classic practitioner workhorse: no parameters
to break, no trends to chase. \emph{Implementation:} global window chosen on
validation (M5: $w=14$; Store: $w=7$; per-series counts favored 28 on M5 and 7
on Store, but the global rule won on validation MAE). \emph{Role:} the simple
method the fancy ones must justify themselves against. It nearly tops M5
forecasting (MASE 1.346 vs.\ LSTM 1.316) and \emph{wins} Store inventory
(2,084.49).

\subsection{Simple Exponential Smoothing}
\label{sec:m-ses}
\emph{Does:} tracks a slowly moving level, weighting recent days more
($\alpha=0.1$ selected on validation). \emph{Why:} the minimal model with
memory --- level without trend or seasonality \citep{hyndman2018}. \emph{Implementation:}
transparent SES, grid-searched $\alpha$. \emph{Role:} smoothing-rung anchor.
SES is the quiet overachiever: second-cheapest on M5 inventory (159.31),
second-cheapest on Store (2,113.82), and winner of 2 M5 sensitivity policies
at short lead with high stockout penalty.

\subsection{Double Exponential Smoothing}
\label{sec:m-des}
\emph{Does:} SES plus a trend component ($\alpha=\beta=0.2$). \emph{Why:} to
test whether explicit trend modeling helps. \emph{Implementation:} Holt's
method, grid-searched. \emph{Role:} cautionary tale. DES over-stocks badly
(average inventory 8.41 on M5, 76.07 on Store) and lands mid-pack or worse on
cost --- trend extrapolation on noisy retail data buys inventory, not accuracy.
It nonetheless wins 5 Store sensitivity policies, which says more about Store
fragility than DES quality.

\subsection{Triple Exponential Smoothing}
\label{sec:m-tes}
\emph{Does:} DES plus a weekly seasonal component (additive Holt--Winters,
$0.3/0.3/0.3$). \emph{Why:} the full classical toolkit in one model.
\emph{Implementation:} transparent additive form. \emph{Role:} the negative
finding. TES is worst almost everywhere: M5 MASE 2.60 and cost 346.68; Store
MASE 2.11 and cost 3,419.88; worst in every archetype. Complexity without
discipline is just expensive noise.

\subsection{ARIMA}
\label{sec:m-arima}
\emph{Does:} models tomorrow as a linear mix of recent values and recent
shocks after differencing to stationarity \citep{box1970,hyndman2018}. \emph{Why:} the
statistical view of autocorrelation that smoothing cannot express.
\emph{Implementation:} fixed ARIMA(1,1,0) after a validation sanity check
(per-series search rejected as overfitting-prone and over-budget); series with
$>85\%$ zeros skip fitting and fall back to Naive. \emph{Role:} linear-memory
representative. Mid-pack accuracy (M5 MASE 1.51; Store 1.45) with a striking
fallback asymmetry: 22.65\% on sparse M5 vs.\ 0\% on dense Store.

\subsection{SARIMA}
\label{sec:m-sarima}
\emph{Does:} ARIMA plus seasonal autoregression and moving averages.
\emph{Why:} to test whether explicit weekly autocorrelation beats SES-style
seasonality on dense data. \emph{Implementation:} SARIMA(1,1,0)(0,1,1)$_7$,
Store-only, full 500 series $\times$ 8 origins (4,000 fits, 4,000 successes,
2,382\,s). An earlier 100-series subset exists but is exploratory-only and
never the headline. \emph{Role:} seasonal-statistical challenger. Store MASE
1.055 --- second-best forecaster --- yet fifth-cheapest on cost (2,282.31):
exhibit B of the forecast--decision gap.

\subsection{LSTM}
\label{sec:m-lstm}
\emph{Does:} a recurrent network with gating that learns nonlinear, long-range
temporal patterns across all series jointly \citep{hochreiter1997}.
\emph{Why:} the neural rung: can shared structure beat per-series statistics?
\emph{Implementation:} one global model per dataset, 28-day lookback,
history-only per-series scaling, chronological per-series validation (rebuilt
during hardening to remove overlapping-window leakage), seed 42.
\emph{Role:} the complexity thesis. It wins forecasting on both datasets and
M5 inventory, but loses Store inventory --- the most important single-model
story in the report (section 4.12).

\subsection{Croston}
\label{sec:m-croston}
\emph{Does:} splits intermittent demand into two hidden sequences --- demand
size when it occurs, and interval between occurrences --- smoothing each
separately \citep{croston1972}. \emph{Why:} general models assume every day
carries signal; on sparse data most days carry none, and Croston refuses to
fit noise to zeros. \emph{Implementation:} M5-only, $\alpha=0.1$
validation-selected, flat 28-day rate forecast. \emph{Role:} intermittency
specialist baseline. Respectable accuracy (MAE 1.08) and the best service level
on M5 (93.9\%), but out-stocked on cost (174.81) by its own corrected
siblings.

\subsection{SBA}
\label{sec:m-sba}
\emph{Does:} Croston with the Syntetos--Boylan debiasing factor
\citep{syntetos2005}: multiplies the rate by $(1-\alpha/2)$ to correct
Croston's over-forecast bias. \emph{Why:} the standard bias-corrected
intermittent-demand method. \emph{Implementation:} M5-only, $\alpha=0.5$.
\emph{Role:} best cost performer of the family (160.66) --- a small algebraic
correction worth real money.

\subsection{TSB}
\label{sec:m-tsb}
\emph{Does:} replaces Croston's interval smoothing with direct probability-of-demand
updating, reacting faster when demand patterns shift \citep{teunter2011}.
\emph{Why:} the modern intermittent-demand alternative. \emph{Implementation:}
M5-only, $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.1,0.3)$. \emph{Role:} best accuracy of the family
(MAE 1.010, MASE 1.369) but second on cost (165.13): within one family, the
accuracy--cost ranking already flips.
