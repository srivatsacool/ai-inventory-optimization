\section{Project Overview and Objective}
\label{sec:overview}

\subsection{Problem context}
\label{sec:problem}
Every retailer runs the same daily gamble. Order too much and cash sits on
shelves gathering dust; order too little and customers walk away empty-handed,
taking their money to a competitor. The first mistake shows up as holding cost,
the second as stockout cost, and the optimal order sits somewhere between them.
Getting that balance right starts with predicting demand --- but it does not end
there. A forecast is a number on a screen; an order is money in motion. This
study is about the full journey from number to money.

Two demand worlds make the problem interesting. In the first, captured by the
Walmart M5 data, most items on most days sell nothing at all: demand is sparse,
spiky, and intermittent, with a 64.5\% zero rate across the studied series.
Classical forecasting tools were built for smooth, every-day demand and often
stumble here. In the second world, a store-item demand dataset, nearly every
item sells every day (zero rate 0.02\%): demand is dense and comparatively
smooth. A method that shines in one world may embarrass itself in the other,
and any serious claim about ``the best model'' must survive both.

\subsection{Why forecast accuracy alone is insufficient}
\label{sec:why-not-accuracy}
Consider two forecasters. Forecaster A misses by two units on average;
forecaster B misses by three. By any accuracy metric, A wins. But suppose A's
errors are all under-predictions on high-margin rush days, while B's errors are
harmless over-predictions on quiet Tuesdays. Fed into a real ordering rule,
B can easily produce the cheaper shelf. Accuracy metrics weight every unit of
error equally; inventory costs do not. They punish stockouts and overstocks
asymmetrically, filter forecasts through safety-stock formulas, and interact
with lead times and reorder rhythms. Judging a forecasting model without running
its forecasts through an inventory policy is like judging an engine without
putting it in a car. This study therefore evaluates every model twice: once as
a forecaster, once as a driver of inventory decisions under one shared,
identical ordering policy.

\subsection{Research question}
\label{sec:rq}
How does the effectiveness of AI-based inventory optimization change as we move
from traditional forecasting models toward neural-network approaches, when
effectiveness is measured at the level of inventory outcomes rather than
forecast errors alone?

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}
The study tests four neutrally framed claims so that any outcome is
informative: \textbf{H1,} accuracy differs significantly across model
generations; \textbf{H2,} accuracy differences move inventory outcomes under a
common policy; \textbf{H3,} sophistication improves inventory outcomes
monotonically (the claim under test --- its rejection would be the finding, not
a failure); \textbf{H4,} patterns hold across both retail environments.
Verdicts appear in \cref{sec:verdicts}.

\subsection{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compare twelve forecasting approaches --- baselines, exponential
smoothing, ARIMA/SARIMA, Croston-family specialists, and a global LSTM --- on
identical footing across two demand environments.
\item Evaluate each model twice: on forecast accuracy and on the inventory cost
its forecasts generate under a shared ordering policy.
\item Separate statistical significance from practical importance using paired
tests, effect sizes, and confidence intervals.
\item Separate robust conclusions from policy-sensitive ones using a 27-policy
sensitivity grid.
\item Publish a frozen, traceable evidence base any reader can audit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}
The study covers univariate daily demand forecasting (no prices, promotions, or
calendar features), a 28-day horizon over eight rolling origins, and a single
family of inventory policies (daily-review order-up-to, lost sales). The M5
analysis uses a stratified sample of 500 series, not the full 30,490 catalog.
SARIMA runs Store-only and the Croston family M5-only, each for documented
methodological reasons. The planned large-language-model phase is designed but
deliberately unexecuted; it appears only as future work (\cref{sec:llm-future}).

\subsection{Research contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
Empirically, the study demonstrates the forecast--decision gap in both
directions within one controlled design: alignment on M5, divergence on Store
demand. Methodologically, it contributes a shared-policy evaluation harness
that makes cross-model inventory comparison fair by construction, a leakage-safe
rolling protocol with scale-free metrics, and a sensitivity-as-verdict
discipline for robustness claims. Analytically, it pairs every significance
statement with an effect size and every headline ranking with a policy-stability
verdict. For reproducibility, the frozen evidence package (number sheet, figure
and table audits, claim traceability) lets a skeptical reader check any number
in this report back to its source file.

\subsection{Datasets at a glance}
\label{sec:datasets-glance}
\textbf{M5 (primary).} Walmart M5 competition data \citep{makridakis2022m5}: thousands of item--store
daily series, 2011--2016. This study uses 500 stratified series over the common
window 2013-01-01 to 2016-05-22. Sparse and intermittent: the archetype split
is 194 highly intermittent, 199 intermittent, 26 moderate, 80 variable, and 1
smooth series. \textbf{Store Item Demand (secondary robustness environment).}
Kaggle Store Item Demand Forecasting Challenge: 10 stores $\times$ 50 items =
500 series, complete panel of 913,000 rows, 2013-01-01 to 2017-12-31, analyzed
over the same common window. Dense: essentially no zero-demand days.
\Cref{fig:rep-m5,fig:rep-store,fig:diversity} show the contrast.

\subsection{What the study establishes}
\label{sec:establishes}
Three things. First, a credible forecast leaderboard on both datasets with
uncertainty properly quantified. Second, a decision-level leaderboard showing
where forecasting skill converts into money and where it does not. Third, a
robustness map telling the reader which conclusions survive contact with
different lead times, service targets, and cost ratios --- and which do not.
The short version is the at-a-glance panel: on M5 the smartest forecaster is
also the cheapest stocker and stays there; on Store demand it is not, and
leadership depends on the policy.
