\subsection{Statistical significance vs.\ practical significance}
\label{sec:sig-vs-pract}
With 112,000 paired errors per comparison, tests have high power to detect
small differences, so \cref{tab:spotlight} reports effect sizes alongside
$p$-values. Two rows illustrate why. M5 LSTM versus TSB: $d_z \approx -0.031$
with Holm-adjusted $p \approx 2\times10^{-10}$. The difference is significant
in the formal sense and negligible in absolute terms (two hundredths of an
MAE unit). Store LSTM versus Moving Average: $d_z \approx -0.29$ with
$p \approx 0$, a difference that is both significant and material at nearly
two MAE units. The same significance label covers a tenfold difference in
magnitude. A $p$-value addresses whether models differ; $d_z$, the
rank-biserial $r$, and the bootstrap interval address whether the difference
matters. The full 91-pair table is \cref{app:pairs}; formal methodology is in
\cref{app:stats}.

\subsection{Pairwise statistical comparisons}
\label{sec:spotlight}
Three patterns hold across all 91 pairs. First, the M5 lead cluster is
genuinely ordered (LSTM ahead of Moving Average, SES, and TSB, each pairwise
significant) but the gaps are operationally negligible; the cluster spans
0.05 MASE. Second, Store gaps are an order of magnitude larger, and every
LSTM pairing is significant and material. Third, MA versus SES on M5 is the
study's reference null case: significant by Wilcoxon, not significant by
Diebold-Mariano (Holm $p=0.113$), with an interval spanning zero. Two strong
simple methods with nothing substantive between them, recorded as such even
at $n=112{,}000$.

\subsection{Inventory evaluation framework}
\label{sec:inv-frame}
The shared engine from \cref{sec:policy} supplies the decision treatment:
every forecast drives the same daily-review order-up-to policy ($L=7$, 95\%
target, $H=1$, $P=5$) with lost sales, where total cost equals holding plus
stockout cost. Rankings below are total cost under that policy; service
level, average inventory, and reorder frequency record how each cost was
composed. Sensitivity of rankings to the policy itself is tested separately
in \cref{sec:robust,sec:sens}.

\subsection{Forecast accuracy vs.\ inventory decision quality}
\label{sec:paradox}
\Cref{fig:scatter} plots forecast error against inventory cost per model. On
M5 the association runs in the expected direction: lower error accompanies
lower cost, with LSTM at the favorable corner. On Store demand it does not:
LSTM is leftmost (lowest error) but mid-ranked vertically, while Moving
Average, SES, and Seasonal Naive sit to its right and below it. The
mechanism is cost asymmetry. The policy charges under-prediction (lost sales
at $P=5$) five times the rate of over-prediction (holding at $H=1$), and
safety stock scales with each model's own error volatility. A forecaster that
averages toward the middle and rarely under-shoots can therefore record lower
cost than a sharper forecaster with occasional shortfalls. Accuracy is a
symmetric criterion; the inventory objective is not.

\subsection{Croston-family findings}
\label{sec:croston-find}
The specialists support their inclusion. On M5, TSB records the family's
lowest forecast error (MAE 1.010, MASE 1.369; fourth overall), followed by SBA
(1.066) and Croston (1.082): adaptivity ranks ahead of bias correction, which
ranks ahead of the original, on accuracy. On cost the order differs: SBA
(160.66), TSB (165.13), Croston (174.81). SBA's debiasing trims the
over-forecast that holding cost penalizes, while TSB's responsiveness carries
somewhat higher stock. Within a single family, then, accuracy and cost
already rank methods differently. Substantively, the family validates the
design choice: on intermittent demand, methods that decline to fit zeros
belong in the comparison.

\subsection{LSTM findings}
\label{sec:lstm-find}
The neural rung partly confirms its premise. As a forecaster, the global LSTM
records the lowest MAE and MASE on both datasets, the lowest archetype MAE on
sparse shapes, and the only sub-1.0 MASE on Store demand. As an inventory
input it is split: lowest cost on M5 (152.83, first in 25 of 27 policies) but
fourth on Store (2,247.45), where its sharper forecasts include
under-predictions that the $P/H=5$ cost ratio penalizes heavily. On
provenance: the LSTM was revalidated on chronological per-series splits after
overlapping-window leakage was identified in the first validation design, so
these figures reflect generalization under the corrected protocol rather than
validation leakage. For practice, the implication is conditional: forecast
quality is established across both environments, while inventory value should
be assessed against the applicable cost ratio.

\subsection{ARIMA findings}
\label{sec:arima-find}
ARIMA occupies an unstable middle position. On dense Store data it estimates
cleanly (4,000 of 4,000 fits, zero fallbacks) yet ranks sixth on both boards
(MASE 1.452; cost 2,320.87), indicating that linear memory contributes little
where weekly structure dominates. On sparse M5 data, 906 of 4,000 estimations
(22.65\%) trigger the greater-than-85\%-zero pre-check and defer to Naive,
concentrated in Highly Intermittent series (\cref{fig:arima-arch}); there are
zero optimizer failures, so every non-skipped estimation converges. The
pre-check is a specified guardrail rather than an estimation failure, and its
asymmetry (22.65\% against 0\%) measures how demand structure conditions
statistical feasibility.

\subsection{SARIMA full-population findings}
\label{sec:sarima-find}
The seasonal challenger performs as specified. All 4,000 Store estimations
succeed with zero fallbacks in 2,382 seconds (approximately 0.6 seconds per
fit): seasonal differencing at $m=7$ is feasible at full population, not only
on the exploratory 100-series subset (MAE 8.31) that preceded it. Final
full-500 accuracy (MAE 8.45, MASE 1.055, RMSSE 1.040) differs slightly from
the subset, a population effect recorded without adjustment. Decision quality
(2,282.31, fifth) lags accuracy (second): improved weekly tracking still
under-protects against stockouts at $P/H=5$. SARIMA was not estimated on M5,
deliberately: seasonal differencing of predominantly zero series lacks
methodological justification, and the Croston rung already addresses that
setting.

\subsection{Model complexity findings}
\label{sec:complexity}
H3 in monotone form is rejected. The most complex classical specifications
record the weakest results (TES last on all boards; DES an overstocking
liability), the mid-complexity linear model sits mid-table, and only the
neural model converts complexity into accuracy, and then into inventory value
on one of two datasets. The simplest rung (Moving Average, SES) wins or
nearly wins three of the four boards. Complexity purchases capability (the
LSTM's cross-series learning is measurable), but value is determined jointly
with demand structure and cost asymmetry.

\subsection{Robustness across inventory policies}
\label{sec:robust}
\Cref{tab:robust} with \cref{fig:heat-m5,fig:heat-store} summarizes
stability. M5: LSTM ranks first in 25 of 27 policies. Its two defeats, both
to SES, occur in one region: short lead time ($L=3$) with high stockout
penalty ($P=10$), where SES's conservative level-tracking incurs less
emergency cover. Store: Moving Average 9, SES 7, Naive 6, DES 5. No model
reaches half the grid; the lowest-cost model is a function of the policy, not
a property of any model. M5 conclusions therefore support deployment under
stated conditions; Store conclusions support policy-conditional selection.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:sens}
The grid interior indicates where leadership changes. Longer lead times
amplify safety-stock differences, disadvantaging volatility-sensitive models;
higher $P/H$ penalizes under-predictors (Naive deteriorates except in
short-lead Store cells where its leanness helps); higher service targets
favor conservative levels (SES gains). Per-archetype M5 costing (55 rows,
\texttt{sensitivity\_by\_archetype\_m5.csv}) confirms the structural pattern:
sparse archetypes magnify each policy effect. Full matrices are \cref{app:sens}.

\subsection{Negative and surprising findings}
\label{sec:negative}
Null and negative results are reported explicitly. (1) TES ranks last on all
four boards and in every archetype. (2) DES carries approximately double the
inventory of the leading methods at lower service than SES. (3) Naive, the
simplest benchmark, wins 6 Store policies. (4) Seasonal Naive is the
third-lowest-cost Store inventory input. (5) The LSTM trails a moving average
on Store inventory cost. (6) Archetype-level WAPE and sMAPE required pooled
construction because per-series averaging is unstable on near-zero-demand
series; see \cref{sec:metrics} for the correction and its rationale. (7) MA
versus SES on M5 is indistinguishable for practical purposes despite
$n=112{,}000$ paired errors (subject to the dependence qualification in
\cref{sec:stats-method}).

\subsection{Consolidated findings and hypothesis verdicts}
\label{sec:verdicts}
\textbf{H1 (accuracy differs): confirmed;} 91 paired tests, nearly all
significant under Holm control. \textbf{H2 (accuracy moves inventory):
confirmed with qualification;} accuracy moves inventory cost but not
monotonically and not in the same direction on both datasets. \textbf{H3
(monotone sophistication): rejected;} TES, DES, and ARIMA trail simpler
methods, and only LSTM complexity pays, partially. \textbf{H4
(cross-dataset consistency): rejected in strong form;} forecasting leadership
transfers (LSTM on both), decision leadership does not (LSTM against Moving
Average), and policy-stability does not transfer at all (stable against
fragmented). The qualified verdict is the contribution: which properties
transfer (forecast skill) and which do not (decision superiority,
policy-stability) are now mapped.
